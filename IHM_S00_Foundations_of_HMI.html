What Is a Human-Machine Interface?Definitions, usability and the interaction loop




The Human-Machine Interface (HMI; in French interface homme-machine, IHM) designates both the part of an interactive system through which a person and a machine exchange information (screens, controls, messages, sounds) and the multidisciplinary field that studies how to design, implement and evaluate it. The field draws on computer science, cognitive psychology, ergonomics, graphic design and sociology; in English-language research it is closely related to human-computer interaction (HCI), which focuses on the dialogue that takes place through the interface.




Usability
"The extent to which a system can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use" (ISO 9241-11).


User experience (UX)
The user's perceptions and responses resulting from the use and anticipated use of a system: emotions, trust, comfort, before, during and after use (ISO 9241-210).


Accessibility
The usability of a system by people with the widest range of abilities, including disabilities: visual, auditory, motor and cognitive.






Usability componentQuestionTypical measure


EffectivenessCan users achieve their goal?Task completion rate, number of errors


EfficiencyAt what cost in time and effort?Time on task, number of clicks


SatisfactionHow do users feel about it?Questionnaires such as SUS (section 05)








The interaction loop: Norman's seven stages of action


Donald Norman models every interaction as a cycle. To act, the user must cross the gulf of execution (how do I do what I want?); to understand the result, they must cross the gulf of evaluation (what happened, did it work?). Good design narrows both gulfs: visible controls and clear labels for execution, immediate and explicit feedback for evaluation.


Gulf of execution: stages 2-4Gulf of evaluation: stages 5-7


Example: paying a bill onlineA wide gulf of execution: the "Pay" button is hidden in a menu labelled "Services". A wide gulf of evaluation: after clicking, the page simply reloads without any confirmation, and the user does not know whether they have been charged. The HTML, CSS and JavaScript of this module are the tools that narrow these gulfs.







02
The Human Side: Perception, Memory, ActionCognitive and motor foundations




Perception: the Gestalt laws


The visual system does not perceive isolated pixels but organised wholes. The Gestalt laws, formulated by German psychologists in the early twentieth century, describe these spontaneous groupings. They explain why a layout "reads" well or badly, before any text is read.






A uniform grid: no structure is perceived, the eye scans dot by dot.




Other laws: continuity (aligned elements are read as a sequence, hence the importance of alignment grids), closure (the mind completes incomplete shapes, as in many icons), and figure-ground (a dialog stands out from the dimmed page behind it).






Memory: the limits of working memory


Working memory holds only a few items at once: Miller (1956) proposed "seven, plus or minus two", and more recent work (Cowan, 2001) suggests around four meaningful units, or chunks. Interfaces should therefore favour recognition over recall (show options rather than asking users to remember them) and group long information into chunks.


00799999001234567812


00799999001234567812


The same 20-digit bank account number: four groups of five are far easier to read, copy and check. This is why card numbers, phone numbers and IBANs are always displayed in groups.






Action: Fitts' law and Hick's law


Fitts' law (1954) predicts the time MT needed to reach a target of width W at a distance D: MT = a + b × log2(D / W + 1). The logarithmic term is the index of difficulty (ID) in bits; a and b depend on the person and the device. Consequences: important buttons must be large and close to where the pointer or thumb already is; screen edges and corners are "infinitely" large targets for a mouse; tiny links in dense text are slow and error-prone, especially on touch screens.


Hick's law states that decision time grows with the logarithm of the number of equally likely choices: T = b × log2(n + 1). Long flat menus slow users down; grouping and progressive disclosure help.


Live experiment: measure your own Fitts' law


Click START, then the target that appears, nine times. The targets combine three widths and three distances. Each trial is plotted against its index of difficulty, and a regression line estimates your personal constants a and b.




Click START, then the target that appears.






Interpreting your resultNine trials make a classroom demonstration, not a scientific measurement: reaction time, practice and the device all add noise. With a whole group's data, the linear trend becomes very clear.







03
Ergonomic Criteria and Design PrinciplesBastien and Scapin, Norman, Nielsen




Decades of research have been condensed into sets of principles used both to design and to evaluate interfaces. The eight ergonomic criteria of Bastien and Scapin (INRIA, 1993) are a reference in French-language teaching; open each criterion to see its definition and an example.




1. GuidanceGuidage
Advising, orienting, informing and leading the user: prompts, grouping, immediate feedback, legibility. Example: the format hint "DD/MM/YYYY" under a date field.


2. WorkloadCharge de travail
Reducing perceptual and cognitive load: brevity, information density, minimal actions. Example: pre-filling the city from the postal code.


3. Explicit controlContrôle explicite
The system executes only explicit user actions, and the user keeps control: cancel, interrupt. Example: no automatic payment without confirmation.


4. AdaptabilityAdaptabilité
Flexibility and consideration of user experience: shortcuts for experts, guidance for novices. Example: keyboard shortcuts plus menus.


5. Error managementGestion des erreurs
Error protection, quality of error messages, correction. Example: "The amount must be between 50 000 and 5 000 000 DZD" next to the field, with the input preserved.


6. ConsistencyHomogénéité / cohérence
Similar choices in similar contexts: same labels, positions and formats everywhere. Example: "Validate" always at the bottom right.


7. Significance of codesSignifiance des codes et dénominations
Relationship between a term or symbol and what it refers to. Example: a red icon for deletion, not for saving; "Delete" rather than "Process".


8. CompatibilityCompatibilité
Agreement between the system and users' characteristics and tasks: their language, their habits, their documents. Example: amounts in DZD with spaces as thousands separators.

04
User-Centred DesignFrom needs to prototypes




User-centred design (UCD, ISO 9241-210) places users at the centre of an iterative process: designs are produced, evaluated with users, then refined, rather than specified once and coded blindly.




1. UNDERSTANDObserve and interview users; analyse tasks and context.


2. SPECIFYPersonas, scenarios, user stories, requirements.


3. DESIGNSketches, wireframes, interactive prototypes.


4. EVALUATETest with users; heuristic review; measure.


5. ITERATECorrect and repeat until the requirements are met.








Personas


A persona is a fictitious but realistic archetype of a user group, built from observed data. It keeps the team focused on real needs ("would Samira understand this?") rather than on the designer's own preferences.




S




Samira, 46, owner of a grocery store in Hadjout. Uses a mid-range Android phone daily (WhatsApp, Facebook), rarely a computer. Keeps her accounts in a notebook.


Goals: know at the end of each day how much she earned; prepare documents for her accountant. Frustrations: small text, English-only applications, forms that lose her data. Context: uses her phone between customers, often with one hand, in a noisy shop.


Design implications: large touch targets, Arabic and French interface, automatic saving, few fields, big clear totals.










Scenarios and user stories


A scenario narrates how a persona accomplishes a goal with the future system. A user story condenses a need into one sentence: As a [role], I want [action] so that [benefit], completed by acceptance criteria that make it testable.


ExampleAs a shop owner, I want to record a sale in under ten seconds so that I can keep serving customers. Acceptance: at most two fields (amount, optional label); the amount field has the numeric keyboard; the daily total updates immediately; works with one thumb on a 360px screen.






From sketch to prototype


Ideas are explored first on paper, then refined at increasing levels of fidelity. Low fidelity is fast and invites criticism of structure; high fidelity is needed to test appearance and detailed interaction. Free tools: paper and pencil, Penpot, Figma (free plan), or HTML/CSS directly once the structure is stable.

The System Usability Scale (SUS)


Created by John Brooke in 1996, SUS consists of ten statements rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), alternating positive (odd items) and negative (even items) wording. Scoring: for odd items subtract 1 from the answer; for even items subtract the answer from 5; add the ten contributions and multiply by 2.5, giving a score from 0 to 100. Across hundreds of studies the average is about 68. Fill in the questionnaire for an application you use daily:




50.0


CautionA SUS score is not a percentage, and it does not tell why users struggle. Combine it with observation.

EX 02
Fitts' and Hick's laws applied
Intermediate


A mobile banking application places its "Transfer" button (48px wide, measured along the direction of movement) at 300px from the thumb's resting position. A redesign proposes a full-width button 120px from the thumb. Take a = 100ms and b = 150ms/bit.


  1. Compute the index of difficulty and the predicted movement time for both designs.
  2. The main menu currently shows 15 equally likely options in a flat list. A redesign groups them into 3 categories of 5 options. Using Hick's law with b = 200ms, compare the decision time of the flat menu with a two-step selection (choose a category, then an option).
  3. What does the second result show about the limits of applying a law mechanically?





1. Current: ID = log2(300 / 48 + 1) = log2(7.25) = 2.86 bits; MT = 100 + 150 × 2.86 = 529 ms

   Redesign: the effective width is now large; with W = 120 (conservative) and D = 120: ID = log2(2) = 1 bit; MT = 100 + 150 = 250 ms, more than twice as fast.

2. Flat: T = 200 × log2(15 + 1) = 200 × 4 = 800 ms

   Two steps: 200 × log2(3 + 1) + 200 × log2(5 + 1) = 400 + 517 = 917 ms



InterpretationFor equally likely, well-understood options, a single flat list is predicted to be faster than a two-level hierarchy, because the logarithm favours one large choice over several small ones. Grouping is nevertheless often better in practice when options are numerous, unfamiliar or have to be searched rather than chosen, since Hick's law models decision, not visual search or comprehension. Laws are tools for reasoning, to be confirmed by testing.

EX 05
Algorithmic warm-up: delivery fees
Foundational


Write the pseudo-code, then draw the flowchart, of the following rule: an online shop charges delivery fees of 400 DZD in the Tipaza wilaya and 700 DZD elsewhere; delivery is free for orders of 8 000 DZD or more; for orders below 1 000 DZD, delivery is refused and a message is displayed. The algorithm reads the order amount and the wilaya, and displays either the fees and the total, or the refusal message.




pseudo-code
READ amount, wilayaIF amount < 1000 THEN    DISPLAY "Minimum order: 1 000 DZD"ELSE    IF amount >= 8000 THEN        fees <- 0    ELSE IF wilaya = "Tipaza" THEN        fees <- 400    ELSE        fees <- 700    END IF    DISPLAY fees, amount + feesEND IF


Order of the testsThe refusal is tested first, because it excludes every other case; then the free delivery, which does not depend on the wilaya. Testing the wilaya first would require repeating the free-delivery test in two branches. In Session 09 you will translate this into JavaScript with if...else.
